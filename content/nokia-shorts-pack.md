## Production Standard (apply to all 8)

- **Safe zones:** keep all text between 12% and 88% of frame height (headline top third, caption bottom third).
- **Hook rule:** the first spoken words and the first on-screen text must land inside 1.5 seconds. No logo, no intro.
- **On-screen text:** ultra-bold sans, max 5 words per card, yellow + white only (matches the thumbnail `NOKIA FELL FAST` treatment for series consistency).
- **Pacing:** change the visual every 3–4 seconds. Slow-pan or zoom the still image — never leave a static frame for more than 4s.
- **Captions:** burned-in word-by-word captions, high contrast, single line.
- **Numbering:** post in the order below. Each ends on a loop or a question so it plays as a series.
- **CTA:** one CTA only, in the pinned comment — not in the script. Keeps retention clean.

## Short 1 — "Nokia Had a Touchscreen First"

**Runtime:** 35s · **Asset:** `02_The_iPhone_Arrives_2007.jpg`

**Hook (0:00–0:03):**
> Nokia built a touchscreen phone before Apple did.

**On-screen beats:** `BEFORE APPLE` → `SO WHY DID THEY LOSE?` → `THE PROBLEM WASN'T THE HARDWARE`

**Spoken (0:00–0:35):**
> Before the iPhone, Nokia engineers were already prototyping touchscreens. So in January 2007, when Steve Jobs walked out with the iPhone, Nokia's engineers weren't shocked by the hardware. They'd built touchscreens. What shocked them was the software. It felt like it belonged on a computer, not a phone. Nokia's phones ran Symbian — an operating system designed for buttons and tiny screens. And that's the part they couldn't copy fast enough.

**Caption:**
Nokia already had touchscreen prototypes before 2007. The iPhone didn't beat them with hardware — it beat them with software. Part 1 of 8.

## Short 2 — "The Memo That Killed Nokia"

**Runtime:** 42s · **Asset:** `05_The_Burning_Platform_Metaphor.jpg`

**Hook (0:00–0:03):**
> This memo destroyed a hundred-billion-dollar company.

**On-screen beats:** `FEBRUARY 2011` → `"BURNING PLATFORM"` → `SALES COLLAPSED BECAUSE OF THE ANNOUNCEMENT`

**Spoken (0:00–0:42):**
> In 2010, Nokia hired Stephen Elop, a former Microsoft executive, as CEO. In February 2011, he sent his employees a memo. He compared Nokia to a man standing on a burning oil platform in the North Sea, who has to choose between burning alive or jumping into the freezing water. The memo leaked. Then Nokia abandoned its own software and partnered with Microsoft's Windows Phone. And that announcement did the damage itself — customers realised the phones they were about to buy were already dead ends. Symbian sales fell off a cliff.

**Caption:**
Elop's "burning platform" memo — February 2011. It didn't just describe the crisis, it accelerated it. Part 2 of 8.

## Short 4 — "It Started as a Paper Mill"

**Runtime:** 33s · **Asset:** `01_The_Peak_Nokia_1100_Era.jpg`

**Hook (0:00–0:03):**
> Nokia started as a paper mill. In 1865.

**On-screen beats:** `1865: PAPER` → `RUBBER BOOTS, CABLES` → `1998: WORLD'S #1`

**Spoken (0:00–0:33):**
> Nokia started in Finland in 1865 as a paper mill. Over the next century it made rubber boots, cables, and electronics. Then in the nineteen nineties, it bet everything on mobile phones. By 1998 it was the largest phone maker in the world. And it held that position for fourteen years. Which means the company everyone calls a tech failure was actually a hundred-and-thirty-year-old survivor that won for longer than most companies even exist.

**Caption:**
1865: paper mill. 1998: biggest phone maker on Earth. This is the part nobody remembers. Part 4 of 8.

## Short 5 — "They Built Three Operating Systems and Killed All Three"

**Runtime:** 40s · **Asset:** `03_Mistake_1_Divided_Company_Infighting.jpg`

**Hook (0:00–0:03):**
> Nokia built three operating systems. They killed all three.

**On-screen beats:** `SYMBIAN` → `MAEMO` → `MEEGO` → `DEVELOPERS STOPPED TRUSTING THEM`

**Spoken (0:00–0:40):**
> Inside Nokia, teams were competing against each other instead of against Apple and Google. Different groups pushed different platforms — Symbian, then Maemo, then MeeGo. Managers were reportedly afraid to bring bad news upward, so leadership never heard how serious it had gotten. So Nokia kept announcing a new software plan, then replacing it, then announcing another one. And developers did the math: why build apps for a platform that might not exist in eighteen months? The app gap wasn't a design failure. It was a trust failure.

**Caption:**
Symbian → Maemo → MeeGo. Three platforms, three abandoned developer communities. Part 5 of 8.

**Hashtags:** #nokia #symbian #meego #productstrategy #shorts

**Pinned comment:** Platforms don't die from a lack of features. They die when developers stop betting on them.

## Short 7 — "Nokia Isn't Dead"

**Runtime:** 30s · **Asset:** `07_Payoff_Nokia_5G_Telecom_Infrastructure.jpg`

**Hook (0:00–0:03):**
> Nokia never actually died. You've just been looking in the wrong place.

**On-screen beats:** `PHONES: SOLD` → `NETWORKS: KEPT` → `5G INFRASTRUCTURE`

**Spoken (0:00–0:30):**
> Everyone thinks Nokia died. It didn't. It sold the phone business, but it kept the part that actually mattered — network equipment. Today Nokia sells telecom infrastructure to carriers around the world, including the 5G networks your phone runs on. The Nokia name still shows up on handsets too, licensed to another company. So the "collapse" was really a company quietly walking out of a market it had already lost.

**Caption:**
Nokia sold phones, kept networks. It's now a 5G infrastructure company. Part 7 of 8.

**Hashtags:** #nokia #5g #telecom #businessfacts #shorts

**Pinned comment:** Losing a market isn't the same as losing the company — if you keep the part that still has a moat.

## Short 8 — "The Real Reason"

**Runtime:** 36s · **Asset:** `00_Official_YouTube_Thumbnail_Nokia_Fell_Fast.jpg`

**On-screen beats:** `THEY WERE RIGHT FOR 14 YEARS` → `SUCCESS MADE THEM SLOW` → `NOBODY DELIVERED BAD NEWS`

> Nokia didn't fail because it was stupid. It failed because it was right for so long that being right became the culture. Success made the company slow, made managers hide bad news, and made leadership miss how far behind the software had fallen. Great hardware couldn't save it, because the problem was never the hardware. So here's the question worth sitting with: what is your thing currently winning at, that's quietly making you deaf to the thing that's coming next?

**Caption:**
The Nokia lesson isn't "don't miss a trend." It's "build a system that delivers bad news to the top fast." Part 8 of 8.
